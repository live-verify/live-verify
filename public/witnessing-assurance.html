WITNESSING

How a witness proves its own honesty

A witness exists so that nobody has to take the issuer's word. That only helps if the witness's own records can be trusted not to have been altered or back-dated. There are three levels of answer, each stronger and more involved than the last.

Status: a design, not a service. Witnessing sits outside the core Live Verify specification. No witnessing organisation operates today, the reference apps have no witness plugin, and any company names or prices on these pages are illustrations.

1. A reputable firm

The witness keeps ordinary records and, when asked, a responsible person signs a statement of what they show. This is how a solicitor's or an archive's records are relied on today.

Strength: simple, cheap, and familiar to courts. Limit: if the firm altered or back-dated a record, nothing outside the firm would show it. Trust rests on the firm's standing and on what it would lose by being caught.

2. A tamper-evident log

The witness still holds the records, but each day it combines all of that day's entries into a single short fingerprint (the root of a Merkle tree) and publishes it. Once a root is public, the witness cannot change, remove or back-date anything beneath it without the mismatch being detectable. For any one record it can produce a short proof that the record was part of a given day's root.

This is the approach web browsers already rely on for Certificate Transparency logs, so the methods and vocabulary are well established.

Where the root is published matters. A single post on one social network can be deleted, and the network can disappear. Publishing the root in several independent places, having it time-stamped by an established time-stamping authority, or having witnesses countersign one another's roots, all make it much harder to dispute.

Strength: the witness's honesty becomes checkable, not assumed. Limit: it proves a record existed by a given day; it does not prove the witness recorded everything it was sent.

3. A public anchor

This is the second level with one more place to publish: the daily root is also written to a public blockchain. Individual records never go there, only the one fingerprint per day.

Strength: the proof no longer depends on the witness, or on any publisher, staying in business. Limit: it adds cost and complexity, and for most disputes it adds little over publishing the root in several independent places.

A public anchor is an optional extra for a witness. Live Verify itself uses no ledger: a check is a plain web lookup against the issuer's own domain.

Which level fits

  • Everyday documents such as receipts and references: a reputable firm is proportionate.
  • Records likely to be contested, or kept for many years: a tamper-evident log with roots published in more than one place.
  • Records that must outlive any one company, or cross jurisdictions that do not trust one another: add a public anchor.

Whatever the level, the witness must make its own lookup at the issuer's domain. A log of what users reported is a tamper-evident record of hearsay.
